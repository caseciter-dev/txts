10. Admittedly,  the  appellants  were  not  parties  in  the  writ  proceedings.  Being
aggrieved by the impugned order, they preferred appeals before the Division
Bench along with an application seeking leave to appeal against the said order.
The  Division  Bench  dismissed  their  leave  to  appeal,  inter  alia,  holding  as
follows:
“17. …….. it is clear that the impugned judgment has
not affected the right of the applicants to pursue their
constitutional  or  statutory  remedy  against  the
registration  of  the  FIR  and  in  such  a  situation,  it
cannot be held that the applicants established a prima
facie or arguable case for granting the leave sought
for. We find that when sufficient material disclosing
commission  of  cognizable  offences  are  furnished
along with the information under Section 66(2) of the
PML Act, 2002, there is no necessity for conducting a
preliminary  inquiry  and  that  the  accused  in  a
corruption case has no legal right to be heard before
registering the FIR and the accused persons are also
not entitled to demand a preliminary inquiry before
registering an FIR in a corruption case. Therefore, the
points  are  answered  against  the  applicants  and
accordingly,  we  find  that  the  applications  seeking
leave to file appeals are liable to be dismissed.”
Consequently, the appeals were also dismissed by the High Court. 
11. Mr. John has forcefully contended that as per  Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v.
Union of India [(2023) 12 SCC 1], the State was obliged to register an FIR as per
communication  made  to  them  under  Section  66(2)  of  the  2002  Act  by
Enforcement Directorate. The appellants, being accused persons, do not have a
right of hearing at the time of registration of such FIR.
4

--- Page 6 ---
12. We are unable to agree to such contention advanced before us. This is not a case
where, on receipt of communication under Section 66(2), the State had registered
a case on its own. On the contrary, the registration of the FIR and commencement
of investigation thereon was pursuant to a judicial order passed by the High
Court. While passing such order, the Single Judge had inter alia observed that the
materials  in  the  communication  by  Enforcement  Directorate  discloses  a
cognizable offence and therefore, it was obligatory for the State to register an
FIR. If the appellants are not permitted to assail such finding in the appellate
proceedings, the same shall come in their way, if an independent proceeding is
initiated, assailing the First Information Report as per law. That apart, the scheme
with regard  to registration  of an FIR  through  judicial intervention  under  the
BNSS has undergone a change from what was envisaged under the Code of
Criminal Procedure. In the erstwhile Code, Section 156(3) did not require the
participation or involvement of the public servant or his superior in exercise of
Magisterial discretion to issue direction for registration of FIR. However, under
Section 175(4) of the BNSS, such a requirement has been incorporated, which
reads as follows: 
“175(4)  Any  Magistrate  empowered  under  section
210, may, upon receiving a complaint against a public
servant  arising  in  course  of  the  discharge  of  his
official duties, order investigation, subject to—
(a)  receiving  a  report  containing  facts  and
circumstances of the incident from the officer superior
to him; and
(b) after consideration of the assertions made by the
public  servant  as  to  the  situation  that  led  to  the
incident so alleged.”
13. Mr. Zoheb Hossain, learned counsel, argues that such requirement would be
5

--- Page 7 ---
hedged by the fact that the offence is committed by public servant in discharge of
official duty, which would not apply to former public servants in corruption
cases.
14. We have referred to Section 175(4) of the BNSS not in reference to the merits of
the case before us, but to elucidate the expansive jurisdiction envisaged in the
new law with regard to participation of a potential accused prior to registration of
FIR. That apart, we are also minded to observe that the Single Judge has not only
directed  registration  of  an  FIR,  which  is  the  mandate  in  Vijay  Madanlal
Choudhary (supra), but also directed commencement of investigation and filing
of final report, which may require consideration of other statutory requirements
under the special law including sanction under Section 17A thereof. 
15. These facts clearly show the appellants had  locus  to prefer appeal  against the
impugned  judgment  and  order  and  their  prayer  for  leave  to  appeal  were
incorrectly turned down.
16. Accordingly, we set aside the impugned  judgment  and  order  and permit  the
appellants to pursue their appeals before the Division Bench. The Appeals are
restored to file. 
17. We make it clear that our observations are limited to the issue at hand, that is, the
locus of the appellants to prefer the appeal, and not an expression of opinion on
merits. We also give liberty to the appellants to seek interim relief in accordance
with law.
18. To enable the appellants to do so, we direct that the investigation in the impugned

shall not be treated as a conclusive opinion expressed on interim relief, and the
Division  Bench  shall  be  entitled  to  fashion  independently  such  relief  in
accordance with law.
19. We are informed that a subsequent ECIR has been registered in respect of the
impugned FIRs. We express no opinion with regard to its validity and keep it
open to be agitated in appropriate proceedings, if instituted in accordance with
law.
20. The appeals are, accordingly, disposed of.

12. We have heard learned Senior Counsels at the Bar and perused
the relevant materials on record. Free and fair elections are a
basic structure of the Constitution. Right to freely participate in
such election by filing nomination papers without hindrance is a
facet of the free and fair election process. It is a constitutional
duty vested on the State Election Commission under Article 243ZA to
conduct and supervise Municipal elections in a free and fair
manner.  Discharge of such duty would necessarily include all steps
to ensure unhindered filing of nomination papers by all contesting
candidates. The power of the constitutional Courts to exercise
judicial review, though deferential in election matters, cannot be
said to be wholly negated when the discharge of such constitutional
duty of the State Election Commission is hampered due to widespread
violence impairing peace and tranquility and thereby skewing a
level playing field in filing nominations. 
13. The philosophy of judicial deference to the control and
superintendence of elections by the Election Commission has grown
6

--- Page 18 ---
from a doctrine of necessity that the election process which is
entrusted in the hands of an independent constitutional authority
be permitted to be concluded within a prescribed timeline and not
be mired in the quagmire of intermediate judicial interference. All
grievances to such election process may be agitated in an election
petition after the process is over. However, when the principal
opposition political party in the State had approached the High
Court even prior to the commencement of the election process and
had placed on record ample representations and materials expressing
serious apprehension and/or instances of use of force and coercion
during filing of nomination papers, the power of judicial review
cannot be muted on the plea of non-interference in election
matters. In such cases, judicial intervention is imperative to
ensure a fair and impartial conclusion to the election process and
cannot be eschewed as “calling in question an election” prior to
its conclusion as noted below in  Election Commission of India
through Secretary v. Ashok Kumar and others [(2000) SCC Online SC
1234]: 
"Any decision sought and rendered will not
amount to "calling in question an election"
if it subserves the progress of the election
and  facilitates  the  completion  of  the
election. Anything done towards completing or
in furtherance of the election proceedings
cannot  be  described  as  questioning  the
election.
Subject to the above, the action taken or
orders issued by Election Commission are open
to  judicial  review  on  the  well-settled
parameters which enable judicial review of
decisions of statutory bodies such as on a
case of mala fide or arbitrary exercise of
power being made out or the statutory body
being shown to have acted in breach of law."

15. In similar vein, noting that instilling confidence in election
process is vital for the maintenance of constitutional democracy

v. State of West Bengal and others [(2018) SCC Online SC 337]
observed as follows:

“16. We hope and trust that in order to

16. These  authorities  above  unequivocally  state  while
constitutional courts would ordinarily not interfere with election
process,  but  their  hands  are  not  tied  when  appropriate
interventions are necessary to ensure that elections are conducted
in  a  free  and  fair  manner  and  are  not  marred  by  extra-
constitutional or illegal means, for eg. muscle power.
