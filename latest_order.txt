1. Petitioners,  who  figure  as  accused  in  a  complaint1 alleging  offence
punishable under Section 138 of the N.I. Act2, impugn a judgment and
order  dated  3rd August,  20263 of  a  Single  Judge  of  the  High  Court4
dismissing their writ petition5.
2. The question that arose for decision in the writ petition before the High
Court and arising in this special leave petition is one and the same. It is
well-captured in the first paragraph of the impugned judgment, reading
as follows:
“1. The short question raised in the present petition is whether an

Asset Reconstruction Company (ARC), which acquires a bank’s

substituted as the complainant in a pending prosecution under
Section 138 read with Sections 141 and 142 of the Negotiable

3. According to Mr. Dave, learned senior counsel for the petitioners, there is
no  authoritative  pronouncement  of  the  Supreme  Court  on  the  said

[Man  Singh  Tusaria  v.  JM  Financial  Asset  Reconstruction

However,  referring  to  the  decisions  of  the  High  Court  in  Indorama
Synthetics (India) Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra9 and Tayal Cotton
Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra10, which respectively considered
Section 446 of the Companies Act11 and Section 14(1)(a) of the IBC12, Mr.
Dave contends that the provisions of sub-sections (4) and (5) of Section
5 of the SARFAESI Act13 should be so read so as to exclude the right of an
assignee to substitute itself as a complainant in the pending proceedings

4. Mr. Dave was further heard to contend that the first respondent, which is
an ARC14, is not the “payee” of the subject cheques and that the High
Court erroneously proceeded to hold that it has become a “holder in due
course” by virtue of the deeming fiction in sub-section (2) of Section 5 of
the SARFAESI Act, overlooking the three cumulative statutory conditions
of Section 9 of the N.I. Act which it did not satisfy; hence, the first
respondent does not qualify to step into the shoes of the original lender.
5. Finally,  Mr. Dave, also refers  to  the  expression  “other  proceeding  of
whatever nature” in Section 5(4) of the SARFAESI Act to contend that the
same ought to be read ejusdem generis the words  “suit” and “appeal”
preceding it; and, if the impugned judgment is left uncorrected, it will (i)
convert every criminal prosecution under Chapter XVII of the N.I. Act into
a  transferable  “financial  asset”  tradeable  amongst  ARCs;  (ii)  permit
strangers  to  the  negotiable  instrument  to  prosecute  drawers  for  a
personal, penal offence; and (iii) render Section 142(1)(a) of the N.I. Act
a strict, non-obstante embargo, on cognizance, a dead letter. 
6. Resting on the aforesaid contentions, Mr. Dave submits that the question
calls for an authoritative pronouncement by this Court.
7. Having heard Mr. Dave, and on perusal of the impugned judgment, we
see no reason to take a view different from that of the High Court; hence,
the same deserves to be upheld. However, we propose to briefly assign
our reasons in support thereof.

guided thereby in arriving at its conclusions.

12. What appears on a bare reading is that Section 5 permits an ARC21 to
acquire the financial assets of a bank/financial institution. In terms of
sub-section (1), inter alia, it could be by entering into an agreement for
transfer, on such terms and conditions as may be agreed upon between
them. Sub-section (2), which serves as the foundation for sub-section (4),
provides  for  substantive  vesting  of  rights.  By  a  deeming  fiction,  on
acquisition  of  the  financial  assets  by  transfer,  an  ARC  becomes  the
lender  and  all  rights  of  the  bank/financial  institution  vest in  it.  Sub-
section (3) ordains that all contracts, deeds, bonds, agreements, etc.
relating to the asset continue to remain in force in favour of the ARC.
While sub-section (4) is the law providing for procedural continuity, sub-
section (5) governs the procedural aspect for the tribunals, courts and
other authority to abide by when approached by an ARC with a request
for  substitution.  Such  substitution,  however,  is  subject  to  the
requirement thereunder that the application be made with the consent of
the originator.
13. To summarise, the scheme of Section 5 is that on acquisition of the
financial assets, the ARC is deemed to be the lender and as a sequel
thereto, all rights, title and interest of the bank/financial institution vest
in  the  ARC.  The  ARC  can,  subject  to  fulfilment  of  the  statutory
requirements,  continue  pending  proceedings  instituted  by  the
bank/financial institution, as if it were the original secured creditor. By
virtue of sub-sections (4) and (5), having stepped into the shoes of the
original secured creditor, the ARC is entitled to pursue the remedies

assets so acquired. The right to continue proceedings would, however,
be subject to compliance with the requirements of the statute governing
that particular proceeding or remedy.
14. Whether, sub-sections (4) and (5) of Section 5, authorise the ARC to seek
substitution  in  a  pending  complaint  under  Section  138,  N.I.  Act
necessarily  has  to  be  answered  by  meaningfully  construing  the
expression  “other proceeding of whatever nature relating to the said
financial asset is pending by or against the bank or financial institution”
in sub-section (4) and “for the purpose of substitution of its name in any
pending suit, appeal or other proceedings” in sub-section (5).
15. In our considered opinion, the principle of ejusdem generis would have
no application whilst interpreting either sub-section (4) or sub-section
(5). Sub-section (4) has an in-built exception clause. When expressly
referring  to  the  SICA22,  Parliament  in  its  wisdom  provided  that  if  a
reference  to  the  BIFR23 abates  under  SICA,  then  sub-section  (4)  of
Section 5 will not save it. However, this exception does not apply to all
proceedings; it applies to only one proceeding, i.e., under the SICA. The
words  “suit, appeal or other proceeding” are not qualified as ‘civil’ or
‘recovery’  proceedings;  on  the  contrary,  the  expression  “other
proceeding  of  whatever  nature” is  deliberately  unqualified.  It  takes
colour from the engrafted exception – the third proviso to Section 15(1),
SICA – which relates to a proceeding before the BIFR, a proceeding that is

neither a suit nor an appeal. The exception, thus, shows that  “other
proceeding” cannot be read ejusdem generis with the words “suit” and
“appeal” and is intended to travel beyond suits and appeals, which are
generally associated with civil proceedings, and to include proceedings
of a different nature, including a complaint under Section 138, N.I. Act
relating to a financial asset. This interpretation is also consistent with the
legislative object underlying Section 5, which is to facilitate acquisition of
financial  assets  and  ensure  continuity  of  the  rights  and  remedies
attached thereto. The fact that a proceeding under Section 138 of the
N.I.  Act  is  criminal  in  form  would  not,  by  itself,  exclude  it  from  the
expression  “other  proceeding  of  whatever  nature”,  provided  that  it
relates to the financial asset acquired by the ARC and was pending by or
against the bank or financial institution on the date of such acquisition.
Sub-section  (4)  of  Section  5,  for  all  intents  and  purposes,  having
provided for a continuity and the words “other proceeding of whatever
nature” used by Parliament being very wide, the same must be allowed
to operate in its full amplitude. Such width is intentional to ensure that
no proceeding relating to the financial asset is lost for want of a party to
prosecute it.
16. However,  we  add  a  caveat  here.  While  a  pending  complaint  under
Section 138, N.I. Act does not abate on assignment to an ARC of the
financial asset/debt under sub-section (1) of Section 5 of the SARFAESI
Act, such assignment does not, per se, make the ARC the payee or the
holder in due course. The ARC, to continue proceedings, must still prove
that  (i)  the  cheque,  since  dishonoured,  itself  was  transferred  and

delivered  to  it  along  with  the  financial  asset  and  not  merely  the
underlying debt; (ii) there has been an endorsement/assignment in its
favour sufficient to constitute it as a holder in due course under Sections
8 and 9 of the N.I. Act; (iii) the ingredients of Section 138 of the N.I. Act
are satisfied; and (iv) there is no attempt to improve upon the case of

“holder  in  due  course”.  Such  question(s)  of  fact  must  obviously  be
decided on the basis of evidence led at the trial.
